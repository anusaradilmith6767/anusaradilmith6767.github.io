Featured project · 2026 – present

SeaGuard

The decentralized safety net. A boat-mounted SOS beacon that passes distress calls from boat to boat over LoRa radio until a shore station hears it.

Visit seaguard.lk → @seaguard.lk on Instagram

Team CS-21 · five students · IIT / University of Westminster Agile team project (5COSC021C) · My role: lead of the “Field facts” research

One button on the boat

Press it and the boat's position goes out as a short radio message. Other boats pass it on until a shore station picks it up and the Coast Guard sets out.

  • SOS button under a clear cover
  • Antenna on top of the body
  • Solar panel tops up the battery
  • Strap mount holds it on the boat

Early concept, about 254 × 100 × 74 mm. GPS + LoRa inside.

The problem

When something goes wrong at sea, nobody notices.

Small boats go out every day with two or three crew and one engine. When the engine dies or the weather turns there is no phone signal, and the nearest boats are too far away to see. My part of the project is the research behind this: fleet data, fisher equipment, and Sinhala/Tamil usability.

49,271operating marine fishing boats in Sri Lanka, 2024
4,200multi-day vessels covered by a VMS tracker
~91%of boats are outside tracking, about 45,000

Sources: Ministry of Fisheries, Annual Fisheries Statistical Bulletin 2024; IOM and Ministry of Defence. The 91% share is our own calculation.

Existing options cost too much for a small boat

Garmin inReach

~Rs 168,500 + ~Rs 6,740 a month

Satellite messenger with an SOS button.

VMS tracker

~Rs 375,000 per unit

Government tracker that shows the boat on a screen onshore.

SeaGuard (target)

Rs 10,000–15,000 per unit

SOS beacon that relays over LoRa radio, boat to boat. Early target.

That makes SeaGuard roughly 25–37× cheaper than a VMS tracker, and about 11–17× cheaper than a Garmin inReach unit before its monthly fee. (Target prices, my own arithmetic.)

Converted at Rs 337 per US dollar (CBSL, 28 Oct 2026). Garmin figure is about US$500 plus about US$20 a month.

We took it to the Coast Guard

They confirmed the gap.

  • Small boats have no distress system. One-day trawlers and dinghies often go out of phone range.
  • Help usually starts too late. Most missions are search and rescue after someone is missing.
  • A boat can go down in minutes. The officer said 3 to 5. Too fast for a phone call.

Their advice: research an automatic trigger (a simple saltwater sensor is ruled out by splashes), and interview fishermen at Dikowita harbour and write down what they need before building more. That is exactly the next phase.
